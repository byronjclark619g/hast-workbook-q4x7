The Brief (read this first)

The 6 a.m. phone call. Codie Sanchez — the business buyer whose book this course draws on — tells the story of a deal that arrived by phone before sunrise. One of her managers called, very excited and very early: he'd found a business to buy. He knew the seller personally — the connection came through his own family's school friendships. The owner was ready to retire, one of his kids had no interest in taking over, and the deal was made on terms far better than anything on a public listing site. Here's the point of the story: the deal was never listed for sale in public. It went from person to person to person, and it stopped at the first buyer whose name someone said. That is how the best deals move. The question is: whose name will people say?

The two-input machine. Getting people to say your name isn't luck. It depends on exactly two inputs:

  1. How many owners you meet.
  2. How many people know you're looking.

Increase either number and leads appear. Increase both and — Sanchez promises, from her own years of doing exactly this — you eventually have more potential deals than you can handle. That's the whole machine. Everything below is a way to raise those two numbers.

Way 1: Tell people you're looking. Every conversation is a free advertisement, if you use it. Real buyers have a one-sentence version of what they're looking for and say it to everyone: "I buy small service businesses in this county — if you ever hear of an owner thinking about retiring, I'd love an introduction." Simple, specific, repeatable. Your student version is just as honest: "I'm studying how local businesses get bought and sold — I'm building a case on a real one this year." Say something like that often, and people start telling you what they hear. People love having a reason to be useful.

Way 2: Recruit the people who hear things first. Some people are in a position where news about businesses that may sell reaches them early. This module calls that position a crossroads:

  • The four professionals: accountants, attorneys, real-estate agents, and financial advisers. An accountant knows which clients are slowing down. An attorney hears about retirements and partner disputes before anyone. Your family may already use some of these people.
  • Business owners themselves. Owners know other owners. The owner you interviewed in Module 3 knows three more like her.
  • Intermediaries: business brokers and bankers — people whose actual job is knowing who's buying and selling.
  • The chamber of commerce: the local club of business owners. The meetings can be boring. But they are exactly where the owners you want to meet gather.
  • Small-business development centers: free, government-supported offices that help small businesses with planning, financing — and, though few people know it, with owners who are thinking about retirement. Free. Most people never learn they exist.

The maintenance rule: keep in touch, so people remember you. A network you build and then ignore stops working within a few months. The fix is a simple rhythm: check in about every six weeks. A short message — "still looking, still interested, hope business is good" — is enough for someone to remember you without annoying them. And one law above all: don't be a taker — someone who only asks and never gives. Send the article they'd find useful. Congratulate them when something goes well. Show interest even when you get nothing from it. People pass deals to people they like helping — the buyer who only contacts people to ask for something teaches everyone to stop answering.

Why this is better than browsing listings. Remember Module 2: the listed market is the small pond with all the fishermen. A network is a group of scouts watching the big pond and reporting back to you. It costs no money. What it takes is consistency — doing the small things again and again.
