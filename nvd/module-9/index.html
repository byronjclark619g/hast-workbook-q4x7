The Brief (read this first)

After the first offer. Module 8 covered the first records, which a buyer asks for before any offer. If the buyer and the seller agree on a first offer, the buyer checks much more. Now the buyer reads more than tax returns. Codie Sanchez lists cash-flow statements (reports that show money coming in and going out), contracts, insurance, legal problems, and employee records (page 96). She gives buyers a long checklist. She also says that many things on it will not matter in your deal (page 96). The deeper look has two jobs:

  1. Check that the money records are accurate.
  2. Find every risk and every debt that comes with the business.

Every deal is different. Sanchez writes, "Every deal is different, so the items on your due diligence list will change" (page 97). Her example is a laundromat where 80 percent of the revenue comes in as coins. Here is what she would check there (page 97):

  1. Count the coins in the machines, two days a week, for a month.
  2. Look at the energy bills.
  3. Ask how often the machines are serviced, and whether the business has a contract with a repair person who comes quickly when a machine breaks.
  4. Check the rent and the length of the lease, and compare the rent with what the neighbors pay.

None of these four would be on a checklist for a heating and cooling company. That company has no coins and no rows of machines. A good checklist starts with one question: what would show me the truth about this kind of business?

How to build a checklist. Module 8 gave you six things a buyer tests: revenue, profit, customers, competitors, employees, and how the work gets done. In this module you add a seventh: technology. Those are your seven groups. Write at least one item for each group. For every item, write four parts:

  1. What to check.
  2. Why it matters in this kind of business.
  3. Where the proof would be: a record, or something you can see from the outside.
  4. Who could help you check it.

Two more warning signs. Module 8 taught one warning sign: the seller's profit is higher than the profit on the tax return. Sanchez names two more on the same page (page 97).

  1. Bad reviews, again and again. Sanchez says these do not end a deal. A buyer can use them to ask for a lower price. But many complaints to a state licensing board may be a reason to stop.
  2. A missing license. Her example is a landscaper in a state that requires a license. The buyer asks to see it, and the seller says he "never needed one." Sanchez treats that as a bad sign.

Licenses in Indiana. Some kinds of work need a license from the state. Plumbing is one. Indiana's license search (mylicense.in.gov) shows whether a license is active. Other kinds of work are licensed by a city. Hammond, for example, requires contractors who work in the city to be registered with the city. It gives its own heating license, and it posts a list of licensed contractors on its website. Some kinds of work need no license at all. If you look and find none, write "no license found," and write where you looked.

Experts. A buyer does not check everything alone. Sanchez uses three kinds of help (pages 97–98):

  1. An accountant reads the money records and looks for numbers that do not match.
  2. An attorney (a lawyer) reads the contracts and checks whether the business has been sued.
  3. A person who knows this kind of business. Sanchez says you do not always have to pay this person. Many owners enjoy talking about their work, and the cost may be the price of lunch. You can also pay fifty or a hundred dollars for an hour of advice. She writes that the hour "might save you tens of thousands of dollars on a deal." Her rule is to never do a deal without help from a person who knows that kind of business.

In Module 4 you listed people like these in your referral network.

Six bigger questions. Sanchez says some checking looks at more than the one business (page 98). These are good questions for the person who knows the trade:

  1. What risks do businesses of this kind face?
  2. What new technology or other changes could change how the business is run?
  3. Do sales come from a few customers or from many?
  4. Does the business depend on one supplier?
  5. Is the area the business serves changing?
  6. Is anything being built nearby that could change sales?

Technology. In Module 6 you named one technology upgrade for your business. Now you ask a wider question: what software and systems does the business use today? Think about how it schedules jobs, sends bills, takes payments, and keeps customer records. A website shows some of this. Can a customer book online or pay online? Some free website-checking pages also list the tools a website uses.

What you can do as a student. You are still not making offers, and no real records will reach you. So you will build the checklist a buyer would use. You will check two items from the outside. You will mark every other item NEEDS RECORDS. You will plan your experts, but you will not contact them for this module.

  • Wolf Lake has been open 31 years. It has fourteen employees and eleven vans. Eight of the vans are older.
