The Brief (read this first)

The seven doors. In Module 1 you learned that owners sell because they get old, tired, sick, or bored. Time to get precise. Codie Sanchez — the business buyer whose book this course draws on — has bought many businesses and talked with many selling owners, and she finds the same seven life events behind the decision, over and over:

  1. Death — of the owner or someone close; the family needs the business handled.
  2. Divorce — life splits, and the business often has to be sold or bought out.
  3. Illness — the owner's health (or a family member's) changes everything.
  4. Money distress — the owner needs cash, or the business is struggling and they're done fighting.
  5. Burnout — the coffee-shop-man problem: decades in, and the joy is gone.
  6. Departure — retirement, moving away, or a new chapter calling.
  7. Falling-out — business partners who can't work together anymore.

Notice something: almost none of these are about the business. They're about the owner's life. That's why a perfectly healthy, profitable business can suddenly have a motivated seller — and why you can't spot most of these from the sidewalk. Signals (Module 2) get you to the door. Only a conversation gets you through it.

Rule 1: You are not there to impress. You are there to listen. Here is how amateurs blow it: they walk in and start explaining — how they'd run it better, what the business is "really" worth, what new technology it needs. To an owner who built the place over twenty years, that's an insult delivered with a smile. The mantra of a good buyer is to ask questions and listen to the responses. You are talking to someone who knows a thousand things you don't. Act like it.

Rule 2: Curiosity is the whole playbook. Good owner conversations don't feel like business meetings. They feel like a genuinely interested person asking about someone's life work — because that's what they are. Questions that open owners up:

  • How did you get started in this business?
  • What did you do before this?
  • What's your favorite part of running it? What's the toughest part?
  • If you had it all to do over, what would you do differently?
  • What should customers know about this place that they don't?
  • And only when the conversation has warmed — Have you ever thought about what comes next for the business?

Notice the shape: past → present → feelings → future. You earn each layer. The "would you ever sell" question is a last-layer question, and with most owners it isn't asked on the first conversation at all.

Rule 3: They're evaluating you right back. Remember Module 2 — the sale is emotional, and sellers pick the person. While you're learning about the owner, the owner is quietly asking: Does this person respect what I built? Would my crew be safe with them? Are they serious or a tourist? Your honesty matters more than your polish. If an owner asks why you're interested, have a real answer ready. And the best predictor they'll use on you is the same one you'd use on anyone: past behavior. How you've shown up before — in jobs, on teams, in this class — is your track record. Small, but real.

Rule 4: Trust takes more than one conversation. An owner's true reasons come out slowly, in pieces, over multiple visits. That's not a problem with the method — that IS the method. Buyers who rush it get politeness. Buyers who return get the truth.

Your guardrails this year: You are a student building a real Deal File, not an adult making offers — so you talk to owners you can reach safely and honestly: owners you or your family already know, owners connected through school or this class, or owners you meet as a customer. You always tell the truth about who you are ("I'm a student studying how businesses get bought and sold"). You'll be surprised — most owners love talking about their business to a young person who's actually interested. You may be the first one who ever asked.
