The Brief (read this first)

Meet the quiet seller. Picture an owner — call him the coffee-shop man. Twenty-five years of unlocking the same door, flipping on the same lights, putting out the same OPEN sign. The shop paid for his kids' college. He knows every regular's order. And lately… the key turns slower. The sign feels heavier. His kids grew up and chose suits over aprons. He's proud of them — but it means nobody is taking over. He has never listed the shop for sale. If you asked him today, he'd probably say he isn't selling. But if the right person walked in with the right offer and a promise to take care of his customers and his crew? That's a different conversation.

The market you can't see. Codie Sanchez — the business buyer whose book this course draws on — tells a story she has repeated in rooms full of business owners for years: she asks owners over forty to raise their hands — then asks who's actively trying to sell. Nearly every hand drops. Then she asks: who would sell if the right offer came along? And most of the hands shoot back up. Every room, every city, same result. That's an informal poll, not a scientific study — keep that straight in your evidence brain — but it points at something real and important: most owners who would sell are not listed anywhere. There is a whole market of willing sellers that is invisible if you only look online.

Why don't they just list it? Four reasons, and they all make sense once you think like an owner:

1. Privacy. An owner does NOT want employees or customers finding out the business might sell. Workers start job-hunting; customers drift. A public listing is a leak.
2. They're busy. Running the business eats every hour. Preparing a sale listing is a project they keep putting off — sometimes for years.
3. It never occurred to them. Many owners assume nobody would want their business, or they've simply never met a serious buyer. The idea of selling arrives when the buyer does.
4. They're not online people. Many longtime owners built their business on handshakes and phone calls. They are not uploading their financials to a website.

So buyers who only browse listing websites are fishing in the small, crowded pond — competing over the businesses that did list. Buyers who learn to find quiet sellers fish in the big, empty one.

What a quiet seller looks like from the sidewalk. You can't read an owner's mind, but you can read signals. Watch for a business that checks boxes like these:

· Open ten or more years — established, survived, proven.
· The same owner has run it for at least five years — long enough to be tired.
· The owner is near retirement age — you learned the math of that wave in Module 1.
· No successor in sight — no kid or junior manager being groomed to take over.
· Not listed for sale anywhere — you checked.
· The owner seems to care about legacy — their name is on the sign, their history is on the wall.

None of these signals is proof the owner would sell. Each one is a clue — and clues stack. A 20-year-old shop with a 66-year-old owner, no successor, and no listing is a very different lead than a 3-year-old shop with a young owner, even though neither has a FOR SALE sign.

One more thing: the sale is emotional. For most owners, the business is decades of their life. They don't want to hand it to just anyone — they want someone who will respect the employees, keep the quality up, and carry the name well. That's why the hidden market opens for people who show up in person, with respect, and it stays closed to strangers mass-emailing "what's your revenue?" Remember the three things a seller wants, from Module 1. The hidden market runs on the second one.

Part B: Practical Application — The Hidden Market Scan (25 points)

Task 1 — Scan your territory

Find five real businesses in Hammond or nearby that are NOT listed for sale anywhere (check — that's part of the work). For each, run the signal checklist: open 10+ years? · same owner 5+ years? · owner near retirement? · successor visible? · listed anywhere (you checked)? · legacy signals? Then note HOW you know each fact — sign, website, review dates, asking someone, walking past. Label clues honestly. "Don't know" is a legal answer; a guess dressed up as a fact is not.

Task 2 — Rank the leads

Practice scenario. Three owners, all made up for practice:

Owner A opened her bakery 4 years ago, age 35, active on social media, just renovated.
Owner B has run his 22-year-old repair shop since it opened; he's 67; his only employee is part-time; the shop has no website; his name is on the sign.
Owner C bought a 15-year-old cleaning company 2 years ago at age 40 and is growing it fast.
