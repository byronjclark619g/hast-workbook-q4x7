The Brief (read this first)

Four things the profit must pay for. In Module 6, the manager test asked one question: is the profit big enough to pay an operator and still pay you? Codie Sanchez, the business buyer whose book this course draws on, checks more than that. Before she buys a business, she makes sure its profit can pay for four things (page 85):

  1. Loan payments. Can the business pay back the money that was borrowed to buy it?
  2. The operator's pay. Is there enough to pay a manager to run the business every day?
  3. Money to grow. Is there money left over to put back into the business so it can grow?
  4. Your pay. After everything else, is there enough profit left to pay you, the owner?

You already know two of the four from the manager test: the operator's pay and your pay. This module adds the other two. In this class we call it the four-things test. Each new thing makes the test harder. A business that passed the manager test can fail this one.

Loan payments. Most buyers borrow part of the price, and the loan has to be paid back every year. Money borrowed from a bank is a loan. Money borrowed from family members is a loan too. It still has to be paid back. Sanchez gives a planning rule for how much to plan for each year (page 59). First, add your pay and the operator's pay. That total is your target income. Then multiply it by 0.25. Sanchez's worksheet adds one-fourth of your target income to cover the yearly loan payments. This is her planning rule, not a bank rule. You will learn how real loans work later in this course.

Money to grow. A business that spends every dollar on its bills and its workers cannot get better. It needs money to fix and upgrade things. Sanchez asks whether there is money left over to reinvest (put back into the business) for future growth (page 85). She does not give a number for it. So in this class we use the technology upgrade you named in Module 6. Money to grow = what that upgrade costs for one year. If the price is for one month, multiply it by 12.

The full test, step by step.

  1. Target income = your pay + the operator's pay.
  2. Loan payments = target income × 0.25.
  3. Money to grow = what the upgrade costs for one year.
  4. Profit needed = target income + loan payments + money to grow.

With this class's numbers from Module 6, target income is $150,000 + $150,000 = $300,000. Loan payments are $300,000 × 0.25 = $75,000. Together that is $375,000, before money to grow. So a business needs at least $375,000 a year in profit, plus the yearly cost of its upgrade.

When you know a business's profit, run the test the same way as the manager test. Start with the profit. Subtract $150,000 for the operator. Subtract $150,000 for yourself. Subtract $75,000 for loan payments. Subtract the yearly cost of the upgrade. If the answer is zero or more, the business passes. If the answer is below zero, it fails.

Your plan can change the test. Two of the four things depend on how you buy and run the business. Sanchez's drawing on page 85 says loan payments count "unless you're paying all cash." It says the operator's pay counts "if you're hiring an operator." So if you pay the whole price in cash, with no loan, there are no loan payments. If you run the business yourself, there is no operator to pay. But then you work in the business every day, as you learned in Module 6. Money to grow and your pay always count. In this class, we count all four things unless a buyer's plan says otherwise.

Look at many to buy one. How many businesses should you look at before you buy one? Here is Sanchez's recommendation (page 62). For every business you buy, look at about 100 businesses quickly. Look at 50 more closely (for example, ask to see their financial records). Study 10 in deep detail. That is how you find the 1 you should buy. She writes that in her group of more than a thousand business buyers, the average buyer looks at more than 30 deals. She also writes that you might need to look at as few as ten businesses, or more than a hundred, before you find the right one (page 62). Why so many? Because the best way to know whether a deal is good or bad is to compare it to other deals (page 63). One business, by itself, tells you very little.

Where your Deal File is now. Every test you have run so far uses what you can see from the outside: walking by, the website, reviews, posted prices, and what people tell you. All of that is the first step: looking quickly. Some businesses in your file got only a quick look. Some you checked for the Module 5 marks. You ran the Module 5, 6, and 7 tests on your front-runner and one other target. In Module 8, one or two businesses move to a closer look. You will learn what records to ask an owner for, and how to check that they are true.

Reading the tests together. A strong target passes every test so far. Is it established? Can it grow? Can it pay a manager? Can the profit pay for everything? For a real target, you still do not know the profit. That is normal at this step. What you do know now is the profit it needs. Next, you find out whether it has it.
