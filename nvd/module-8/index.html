The Brief (read this first)

What due diligence is. Due diligence is the process of checking a business before you buy it (page 84). Codie Sanchez writes that when a business passes the screening tests, it is time to begin due diligence (page 91). In this course, Modules 5, 6, and 7 were those tests.

When the records looked right. A few years ago, Sanchez and her partners looked at a cannabis company in Oakland, California (pages 92–93). It had served the medical market for years. Later it also sold for non-medical use in California, Oregon, and Arizona. Those states made cannabis legal under state law. The federal government, which makes laws for the whole country, has not made it legal.

The buyers checked the tax returns, the lists of products on hand, and the profit-and-loss statements. A profit-and-loss statement is a report that shows revenue, expenses, and profit. Everything matched. Revenue had doubled in one year. They invested $12 million and owned most of the company.

A few months later, the CEO, the person running the company, called. The company was two weeks from running out of money. The buyers studied the records again. The CEO had used company money for his own personal life. He had also paid family members large salaries for jobs that did not exist. Sanchez told him he had taken more than a million dollars. They fired him and sued him. He sued them back, and he lost. The company survived, and a larger company bought it later.

Her lesson: the bigger the investment, the deeper your checking should go. Do not assume anything is true without checking it (page 93).

Six things to test. Before any offer, a buyer tests what the seller has said about six things (page 94):

  1. Revenue
  2. Profit
  3. Customers
  4. Competitors
  5. Employees
  6. How the work gets done

Four ways a seller may not tell the truth. Sanchez names four ways (page 94):

  1. Lie. The seller says something the seller knows is false.
  2. Leave things out. Everything the seller says is true, but the seller does not tell you something important.
  3. Make things confusing. The seller gives you information in a way that makes it hard to understand.
  4. Not know the truth. The seller says something false but believes it is true.

The first records. A buyer who has not yet made an offer asks for a short list of records (page 94):

  1. Three to six years of financials (the business's money records: revenue, expenses, and profit)
  2. Expenses by year
  3. Revenue by year
  4. A list of the business's assets and equipment
  5. Photos of the location
  6. The lease
  7. A short overview or summary of the business

Keep this first request short, because the seller is still running the business (page 95). Before a seller shows any records, the buyer usually signs an NDA, or non-disclosure agreement. An NDA is a written promise to keep the seller's records private (pages 62 and 95).

Which number do you use? Sometimes the profit a seller claims is different from the profit on the tax return. A tax return is the report a business sends to the IRS, the U.S. government's tax office. Sanchez's rule is to use what the seller reports to the IRS, not what the seller claims to make (page 97).

Typical numbers. Sanchez tells buyers to use data about an industry when they start due diligence (page 95). A typical number tells you what businesses of one kind usually earn or spend. You can find typical numbers in YouTube videos, podcasts, online articles, and reports from trade associations. A trade association is a group that companies in one industry join. A typical number is not this business's number. But it can tell you whether a seller's story sounds possible. Ask three questions about every number: Who said it? What does it measure? Is this person selling something? Some sources subtract the owner's pay before they call a number profit, and some do not.

What you can do as a student. A real buyer asks for records after the tests and before an offer. You are building a real Deal File, but not making offers yet, as you learned in Module 3. So you will not send an actual records request, and no real records will reach you in this module. You will practice on made-up records. For your real businesses, you will check what you can from the outside and write the request you would send. If a buyer and seller later agree on a first offer, the buyer asks for many more records and brings in experts such as an accountant. Module 9 covers that.

Task 1 — Sort ten made-up seller statements

For each one, answer two questions:

  • Which of the four ways is this?
  • What tells you?

1. Statement 1

A car wash owner says, "We made $120,000 in profit last year." The tax return she filled out shows $70,000.

2. Statement 2

A sign company owner shows three good years of records. Last week his largest customer told him it is leaving. He does not mention it.

3. Statement 3

A bakery owner sends 60 pages of receipts in no order, with no totals. When the buyer asks for yearly revenue, she says, "It is all in there."

4. Statement 4

A print shop owner says, "We are doing great." He believes it. His bookkeeper has been recording many sales twice, and he does not know.

5. Statement 5

A gym owner says, "We have 900 paying members." She knows that 450 people paid last month.

6. Statement 6

A pizza shop owner gives the buyer the rent amount. She does not say that the lease ends in four months and the landlord will not renew it.

7. Statement 7

A towing company owner shows one line called "business costs." It mixes pay, loan payments, and his family's car payments. When the buyer asks him to split the line, he talks about something else.

8. Statement 8

A tire shop owner says profit went up this year. She does not know that she forgot to count a large tax bill. She believes her number.

9. Statement 9

A dry cleaner says, "Nobody else does this work in town." A new dry cleaner opened two blocks away last month, and he drives past it every day.

10. Statement 10

A hardware store owner gives the buyer a list of employees. The store manager has told her he will quit when the store is sold. She does not mention it.

Step 2 — Compare with typical numbers. Use your Part A Question 5 evidence. Revenue needed is how much revenue the business would need to reach its profit needed, if its profit percent were typical. Revenue needed = profit needed ÷ typical profit percent. Write the percent as a decimal (10% is 0.10). Example, all made up: a business needs $400,000 in profit, and typical profit is 8%. $400,000 ÷ 0.08 = $5,000,000 in revenue.

Do not guess a profit number. A typical number is not this business's number. Revenue needed tells you how big the business would have to be. It does not tell you what the business earns.

Say you would sign an NDA. Write NOT SENT at the top. Do not send it. A real buyer sends this after the tests and before an offer.
