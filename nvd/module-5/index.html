The Brief (read this first)

Buying a business is a long commitment. You'll live with this choice for years — so serious buyers check a business carefully before they get too excited about it. Checking a business this way is called screening. Here is a different way to think about it that makes screening easier: you are not really buying "a lawn company" or "a laundromat." You are buying an income stream — money that keeps coming in, month after month, from the work the business does. A laundromat, a car wash, and a bookkeeping office do very different work. But for a buyer, each one is the same kind of thing: a way to bring in money every month. Screening asks: how steady will this money be in the future?

The established-business screen. The first test looks for four signs. We call them marks. Codie Sanchez, the business buyer whose book this course draws on, says she checks every business she considers for these four marks. A business that shows all four is the kind she looks for. Here is the surprising part: two of the four marks look like weaknesses until you learn to see them the way a buyer does.

Mark 1: Low-tech. The business hasn't kept up with easy modern tools. The website looks very old — or doesn't exist (you can find examples all over your town). No online reviews. Paper receipts. A paper appointment book instead of scheduling software. A beginner sees an old-fashioned business. A trained buyer sees room to grow: every easy upgrade the current owner never made — online booking, review pages, text reminders, digital payments — is growth the next owner gets almost free. A business already running perfectly has nothing left to improve; you would pay full price for a business with nothing left to add. Low-tech means the current owner succeeded without the easy tools. Imagine what it does with them.

Mark 2: Established. Five years old at minimum — ideally a lot more. An established business has loyal repeat customers, a good reputation, and a known place in the community. People even use it to give directions: "turn right after the pack-and-ship place." That kind of business has survived recessions, competitors, and bad winters, and survival is evidence: the longer something has already lasted, the longer it's likely to keep lasting. Compare that with a new, exciting business that everyone is talking about. Attention brings in competitors, and a two-year-old business hasn't proven it can survive anything yet.

Mark 3: Weak competition. This does not mean your target is weak. It means its competitors are doing a poor job. Ask yourself: when did a plumber last show up on time to your house, send an appointment reminder text, or follow up afterward to check the work? If every business of that type is doing a poor, old-fashioned job, then small, cheap fixes — being on time, answering the phone, sending reminders — make your business the obvious choice in town. You don't need to be the best in the world when the other businesses are doing so little.

Mark 4: Simple. You can explain how the business makes money to an eight-year-old: "People with dirty cars come here, drink a coffee, and we make their cars look new." No secret formulas, no science lab, nothing hard to understand. Simple businesses are easier to learn, easier to run, easier to improve, and easier to sell someday. Complicated businesses are where beginners lose the most money.

Reading the screen. The four marks work together: established proves that customers really want it, and have for a long time. Simple means you can actually run it. Low-tech and weak competition are the room to grow — growth that a new, hard-working owner can use. A target that passes all four is not only safe — it is ready to grow. If a business misses two or more marks, treat that as a serious warning, no matter how much you like the owner.

One warning: this screen is the FIRST test, not the whole judgment. Passing it means "keep investigating," never "buy it." What the money numbers must look like comes later — and that test will be strict.

Task 1 — Practice on a made-up case

Practice scenario, all made up:

"Duneland Alterations" has done clothing repair and tailoring in the same strip mall for 19 years. Same owner the whole time. No website — customers call a landline or walk in. Two competitors in the county: one has a voicemail box that's full; the other closed last year. The business model: people bring clothes that don't fit, pay by the job, pick them up Thursday.

Run the four-mark screen on Duneland Alterations. For each mark — low-tech, established, weak competition, simple — write PASS or FAIL plus the evidence from the scenario that decides it. Then one sentence: what would a new owner's first three cheap upgrades be?
