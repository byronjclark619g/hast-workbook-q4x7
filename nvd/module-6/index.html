The Brief (read this first)

Established is not enough. The test in Module 5 asked: has this business proven it can last? A business can pass that test and still be a poor buy. Its money might come in only a few months a year. It might lose most of its customers when times get hard. It might be too small to pay anyone but the owner. So Codie Sanchez, the business buyer whose book this course draws on, runs a second test on every business that passes the first one. The first test shows the business is old and steady. The second test shows whether it has room to grow. We call it the growth test. It has four checks.

Check 1: Steady money. Sanchez wants a business where the money comes in regularly and you can predict it. Good examples: customers pay every month for a plan, sign a yearly service contract, or pay for a membership. The money should also come from many different customers, not one or two big ones. The opposite is uneven money. Some businesses do the work first and then wait and hope to get paid. Others make most of their money in one season or at special events. Sanchez's examples of uneven money include event services, seasonal stores, art galleries, consulting firms, and new tech companies. Her rule: she buys an uneven-money business only if she knows she can change it to steady money in the first ninety days after she buys it.

Check 2: Holds up in hard times. A recession is a time when the whole economy slows down. Many people lose jobs or earn less, and families cut back on what they spend. Some businesses lose most of their customers in a recession. Others keep them. Sanchez explains it with two examples. If money is tight but your toilet is overflowing, will you still pay to fix it? Yes. That business holds up in hard times. If money is tight and your expensive custom picture frame breaks, will you pay for a new custom frame? Probably not. You will buy a cheap one at a big store, or go without. That business does not hold up. Ask: when money is tight, do people still need this?

Check 3: Room to raise prices. Can the business charge more? Sanchez writes that most small businesses charge 30 to 300 percent less than they could, and only about one-third of owners raise their prices every year (page 88). Many owners never raise prices, even when their own costs go up. She says prices should go up as costs rise and as the business adds value for customers. A business that has not raised prices in years has room to raise them. (Raising prices still has to be done carefully. Some customers may leave. The goal is that the business earns more in total.)

Check 4: Useful technology to add. In Module 5, the low-tech mark asked: is this business old-fashioned right now? This check asks a harder question: is there a specific upgrade that would bring in more money or save time? Sanchez's examples include billing software, a computer program that keeps track of every customer, online booking, and automatic reminder texts. These tools let the same business serve more customers, faster, and charge more. To pass this check, you must be able to name the upgrade and say what it would do.

A story from the book (pages 89–90). Sanchez tells how she used the growth test on a business she bought. She found an online learning business that looked neglected — the owner seemed tired of running it. Before contacting the owner, she estimated from the outside that it earned about $100,000 a year in revenue and about $20,000 in profit. Then she ran the four checks. Steady money: customers paid every month. Hard times: she says people often keep learning, or learn more, when the economy is bad. Prices: it charged $29 a month, which she thought was far too low. Technology: it had no social media and no automatic chat on its website. She bought it, and the owner stayed for three months to teach her team the business. Then she raised the price to $299 a month, or $1,000 for a full year. She reports that she lost 3 percent of her customers and that the business's yearly revenue went up. The story shows the four checks used in order on a real decision. But look at the profit: about $20,000. On its own, this business would fail the manager test below. It worked for Sanchez because she already owned a media company with a team to run it (pages 89–90). The manager test depends on who is buying.

The manager test. This is the strictest test so far. First, a reminder and a new word. You learned profit in Principles of Entrepreneurship: profit = revenue − expenses, the money left after the business pays all of its costs. An operator is a full-time manager who runs the business day to day, so the owner does not have to. Sanchez's rule is simple: if the profit is not big enough to pay an operator and still pay you, you are buying a job for yourself, not a business. In this class we use a working target: a business needs about $300,000 a year in profit. About $150,000 pays the operator. About $150,000 goes to you, the owner. Why $150,000? Government wage numbers show that a general manager in the Chicago area, which includes Northwest Indiana, earns about $144,000 a year on average (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, May 2025). We round that to $150,000. In Task 3 you will look up manager pay for your own target's kind of business. To run the test, start with the profit, subtract $150,000 for the operator, then subtract $150,000 for yourself. If the answer is zero or more, the business passes. If the answer is below zero, it fails.

Failing the manager test does not make a business bad. It tells you what you would really be buying. Sanchez says she ran her first business herself, and many owners start that way. But if a business fails the manager test, you must know this before you buy: you would be working there every day to earn its profit. Decide on purpose, not by surprise.

Reading the tests together. A strong target passes the established-business test (Module 5), the growth test, and the manager test. Even then, passing means "keep investigating," never "buy it." The next module adds more things the profit has to pay for, and after that you will learn how to check the seller's records.
