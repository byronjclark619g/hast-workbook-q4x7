The Brief (read this first)

The hard way and the overlooked way.

Most people think being an entrepreneur means inventing something and starting from nothing. Here's what starting from nothing actually looks like: on day one you have zero customers, zero revenue, and zero reputation — and the bills start anyway. Most new businesses never survive long enough to fix that.

There is another path most people never consider: buy a business that already works. On the day you buy it, it already has customers, employees who know the job, equipment, a reputation, and — most importantly — money coming in from day one. You skip the hardest, most dangerous years of a business's life: the beginning. You're not betting it will work someday. It already works. The question is only whether you can keep it working — and make it better.

Why would anyone sell a working business? Because owners get old, get tired, get sick, or get bored — and most of them have nobody to hand the keys to. Across the country, a huge share of small businesses are owned by people at or near retirement age, and most have no plan for who takes over. Their kids want different careers. Big companies don't want businesses this small. So good, profitable businesses close every year — not because they failed, but because nobody showed up to buy them. That's the opening. A prepared buyer — even a young one — can be the person who shows up.

What does a seller like that actually want? Usually three things: (1) to finally stop — retire, rest, move on; (2) to see the thing they built keep going — their name, their workers, their customers taken care of; and (3) a fair payday, which doesn't always mean all the cash up front. Remember those three. The whole year runs on them.

Your Deal File. Real buyers don't keep a deal in their head — they keep a file: every business they're watching, every fact they've verified, every person they've talked to, every number they've checked. In this class, a claim without evidence in your file doesn't exist. That's not a school rule — it's how actual dealmakers avoid losing real money.

Task 2 — The two paths

Read the case, then answer with numbers. Show your work — the answer alone earns nothing.

It's March. You want to be in the lawn-mowing business this season (mowing season here runs about 28 weeks, April through October).

Path A — Start: Spend $2,800 on used equipment. You have zero customers. Knocking on doors, a hustling beginner picks up about 3 new weekly customers per week of effort.

Path B — Buy: A retiring owner two towns over will sell his route: 45 weekly customers at $35 per cut, his equipment included. He's asking $30,000 — but he'll take $6,000 down and the rest in monthly payments out of the route's earnings, because he wants his customers taken care of.
