The Brief (read this first)

Quick — picture a "successful business." Did you picture an app? A sneaker brand? Something with a famous founder?

Here's the secret most people never learn: the businesses most worth wanting are usually the ones nobody pictures. This module gives you three tests and a size map for spotting them.

Test 1: Is it essential? An essential business sells something people need, in good times and bad — not something they only buy when they're feeling rich. Broken pipes get fixed in a recession. Lawns keep growing. Laundry piles up. Cars need washing, pets need grooming, books need keeping. These businesses rarely make the news. Their owners get rich quietly — remember the "quiet owners" from Module 1? This is where they live. A business like this is sometimes called "boring." Boring is a compliment. Boring means dependable.

Test 2: Is it a survivor? There's a famous idea, made popular by the investor and writer Nassim Taleb, called the Lindy effect: the longer something has already lasted, the longer you should expect it to keep lasting. A rule of thumb, not a law of physics — but a powerful one. Think about what a 30-year-old business has already survived: recessions, new competitors, rent increases, three decades of winters. Its age isn't just a fun fact. Its age is evidence. A one-year-old startup hasn't proven anything yet; a thirty-year-old plumbing company has proven almost everything.

Test 3: Is it simple? If you can explain how a business makes money in one sentence — "people pay them to mow lawns" — you can understand it, check it, and run it. If it takes a whiteboard and twenty minutes, be careful.

The size map. Businesses come in sizes, and the size changes everything: the price, the risk, and the job the owner does. One experienced buyer sorts them into tiers, roughly like this:

These numbers are one practitioner's rule-of-thumb map, not government data — but the pattern is what matters. Most first-time buyers start at Tier 1 or 2. And here's the beautiful part: almost all the professional buyers with big money compete for Tiers 3, 4, and 5. The small end of the map — the laundromat, the two-truck landscaping company — gets overlooked. Less competition for buyers means better deals. In fishing terms: plenty of fish, surprisingly few fishermen.

Put the three tests and the map together and you have something most adults never build: a way to look at any business in town and judge whether it's worth wanting — before you ever ask what it costs.

Task 2 — The Tale of Two Businesses

Practice scenario. The numbers are made up for practice — the thinking is real.

Business "Flash" is 14 months old. It's an app-based snack-delivery startup. It's growing fast and gets talked about at school — but it has lost money in both of its years so far.

Business "Granite" is a landscaping company that has been open 32 years. Last year it brought in $1,100,000 in revenue and spent $900,000 to run the business — including the wages of its 8 employees. It earned about the same profit in each of the last three years.
