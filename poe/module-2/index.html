The Brief (read this first)

Last module you learned there are two ways to get money: earn it with your time, or own an asset that earns. But if you want to own a business, there are actually two doors into ownership — so you really have three paths in front of you:

Path 1: Stay employed. Take a job. Safe and steady, but you learned in Module 1 what the ceiling looks like.

Path 2: Build one from scratch. This is called a startup — a brand-new business someone starts from nothing. Startups get all the attention in movies and on social media. But every startup shares one hard fact: on day one, its revenue is zero. No customers. No workers. No reputation. Nobody knows it exists. The owner spends money for months — sometimes years — before the business earns any profit at all. Many never make it that far.

Path 3: Buy one that already works. An existing business already has customers who pay, workers who know the job, and a name people in town trust. On day one of new ownership, the revenue is already flowing. You are not gambling that people will someday want the product — they are already buying it.

Be honest about the price of Path 3. Buying a business is not a get-rich-quick trick. Codie Sanchez — the buyer of many small businesses whose book this course draws on — tells beginners to plan on a timeline like this: a few months just to learn, most of a year to find and close the right deal, and about a year after that to get the business running steady under its new owner. That's one experienced buyer's rule of thumb, not a law — but call it two years, about the same time as a college degree program. And owning is real work: employees, customers, suppliers, problems. A job is hard. Starting a business is hard. Owning one is hard. You don't get to skip "hard" — you only get to choose which hard, and what it pays you.

Buy a system, not a job. Here is the mindset that separates serious buyers from everyone else. The goal is not to buy yourself a place to work — it's to own a business that runs and pays you whether or not you're standing in it. (Remember Omar's vacation from Module 1? Same idea, bigger scale.) Sanchez puts it bluntly: if the profit isn't big enough to pay a skilled operator to run the business AND still pay you, you're buying a job, not a business. An operator is a full-time manager who runs the business day to day so the owner doesn't have to. In this class we'll use a working target: a business earning around $300,000 a year in profit — roughly $150,000 pays the operator, and roughly $150,000 goes to the owner. So what does the owner do all day? The real work of ownership: watching the numbers, making the business better, and going out to find the next business to buy. Plenty of owners run their first business themselves at the start — that's a fine way in. But the plan should always be: own the system; don't be the system.

Now the evidence — why this door is open. All over the world, business owners are getting older, and their kids often don't want the family business. The plan for who takes over a business when the owner leaves is called succession. Here is a true, verified story: Hidekazu Yokoyama, age 73, spent thirty years building a successful shipping-and-logistics company in northern Japan. When he was ready to retire, his children didn't want it. His employees didn't want it. So he gave the business away — a profitable company, worth real money, handed over for free — just so it wouldn't close. Reporters at The New York Times who told his story in 2023 found that the average business owner in Japan is about 62 years old, and nearly 60 percent of Japan's businesses have no plan at all for what happens when the owner stops.

America is walking the same road. Here's a story from Sanchez's own family: her uncle built a plumbing company in Phoenix that earned millions over several decades — it paid for boat trips, a small airplane, a cabin in the mountains. When he got old and sick, he didn't sell it. He didn't hand it to anyone. He slowly shut it down and paid money to close it properly. The customers, the team, the systems — gone. Nobody was standing at the door ready to buy.

That is the gap. Good businesses with no successor either close forever or sell cheap. Someone willing to learn the skill of buying and running one can be the succession plan — keep the business alive, keep the workers employed, and own the asset. That someone could, one day, be a person who started paying attention in high school.

Part B: Practical Application — Two Doors, One Town (25 points)

Task 1 — The Two Doors Math

Practice scenario. The numbers are made up for practice — the thinking is real.

Dana builds. Dana starts a brand-new cleaning company from scratch on January 1. She spends $8,000 up front on equipment and advertising before her first customer. Over her first year, customers slowly find her: the company brings in $30,000 in revenue and spends $18,000 running the business (supplies, gas, a part-time helper) — not counting the $8,000 she spent up front.

Miguel buys. Miguel buys an established cleaning company on January 1. It already has steady customers and a crew of 10 cleaners. After every expense — the cleaners' wages, supplies, gas, insurance — the business earns a profit of $25,000 per month, and it earns that in every month of the year. Miguel does not clean houses and does not run the office. Out of the profit, he pays an experienced operator $150,000 per year to run the business day to day; the rest is his. Miguel spends his time watching the numbers, improving the business, and looking for a second business to buy.
