The Brief (read this first)

Your buy box says what kind of business you want. Now you will pick one real business of that kind and find out as much as you can about it. You will not ask the owner for anything. You will use only what anyone can find.

Due diligence. Codie Sanchez calls this kind of checking due diligence: "the process of evaluating the business before buying it" (page 84). In plain words, due diligence means checking a business carefully before you buy it. You do not believe a claim until you have checked it.

Lisa's story. Lisa Song Sutton owned several businesses in Las Vegas. In 2021 she wanted one more: a business that makes money in good times and bad. She asked herself what people keep paying for, month after month. She thought of the small mailbox she rented at a pack-and-ship store (a store that packs and ships boxes and rents mailboxes). She almost never used it, and the store still charged her about $20 every month (page 82). Here is what she did next.

  1. She asked an owner. A friend owned three pack-and-ship stores. She asked him, "If you could do anything different to make your store more profitable, what would it be?" He answered, "Add more mailboxes." He explained that shipping is busy only in some seasons, and mailbox rent comes in all year (page 82).
  2. She did more research. She learned three things: the mail business was growing 5.3 percent a year; new businesses need mailboxes, and new businesses were opening in her city; and the stores near her had rented every mailbox and had long waiting lists (page 82).
  3. She worked inside a store. She made a deal with the owner of the store she used. She would follow him for two weeks and write down how every job in the store is done. She would give him those written steps, pay him $10,000, and promise not to open a store in his area. For two weeks she opened the store in the morning and closed it at night. She packed boxes, sorted mail, and helped customers. If she hated the work, she would know this business was not for her (page 83).
  4. She chose places with no competition. She opened two stores in neighborhoods that had no store like hers (page 83).

Her stores made a profit in the first year. They had $300,000 in revenue in the second year and $500,000 in the third year (page 84). Lisa opened new stores. She did not buy one. The checking is the same either way.

One more detail. The store owner told Lisa that his store had $1 million a year in revenue (page 83). That was something an owner said. Module 1 has a name for that: a clue.

Clue and proof. Module 1 taught you the difference. A clue is a sign that something may be true. Proof would take the real records. In this module, every finding you write down gets three things: where you found it, the date, and the word clue or proof. Almost everything you find from the outside is a clue. That is normal. A good buyer knows which findings are clues and which are proof.

A market gap. A market gap is something customers need that the businesses near them do not give, or do not give well. It can be the hours, the wait, a service, the language, or how hard it is to reach someone. (Module 2 taught the succession gap: owners with no one to take over. A market gap is about customers.) Sanchez gives an example. She asks when you last had a plumber who came on time, who billed you automatically, and who sent a text afterward. Her answer is never. She calls those "three easy fixes" that make a business you buy better than its competitors (page 87). Each fix is a market gap.

First choice and second choice. You will search for real businesses of your buy-box type in your buy-box area. You will pick the one that looks best to you. That is your first choice. You will also pick a second choice, because a buyer who looks at only one business has nothing to compare it with.

How to estimate size from the outside. An estimate is a number you calculate from evidence. It is not a guess, because you can show where every part came from. It is still a clue. You cannot see a small business's records, so you look for numbers about businesses like it. Here are four places to look.

  1. Typical numbers for that type of business. Some websites publish the average revenue and profit for a type of business. Example: a business-for-sale website's report on plumbing businesses says the average plumbing business in the United States had $1,680,532 in revenue and $218,469 in profit in 2021. That is a profit of 13 percent (BizBuySell, "Plumbing Business Profitability & Financial Benchmarks," read November 15, 2026; the page shows 2020 and 2021 for free).
  2. Businesses of that type that are for sale. In Module 6 you found a real listing. Listings often show revenue and profit. Three or four listings of the same type give you an idea of the usual size.
  3. A big company that does the same kind of work. Companies that sell stock to the public must publish a report every year. The report gives the company's revenue. Many reports also give the number of locations.
  4. Trade groups. A trade group is an organization for businesses of one type, such as plumbers or landscapers. Some post numbers about their industry. Many keep their numbers for members only.

The "per" method. Find a number per something you can count: per location, per truck, or per worker. Then multiply by what you can count at your business.

  • A made-up example: a big company reports $600,000,000 in revenue and 400 locations. $600,000,000 ÷ 400 = $1,500,000 per location.
  • Another made-up example: a listing shows a company with 5 trucks and $1,500,000 in revenue. $1,500,000 ÷ 5 = $300,000 per truck. Your first choice has 6 trucks. 6 × $300,000 = $1,800,000.

Different sources give different numbers. Write your estimate as a low number and a high number. An AI tool can suggest where to look. Its numbers are clues until you find the page they came from.

Keep a search record. For every search, write what you searched for, where you looked, the date, and what you found. Take a screenshot. "I could not find it" is an honest result when your search record shows where you looked.

Can it pay an operator? Module 2 taught that an owner who does not want to work in the business every day must pay an operator. Your buy box has a row for the least profit you need each year. Use it with a typical profit percent. (Module 6 called a profit percent the margin.)

  • Revenue needed = least profit ÷ typical profit percent. Write the percent as a decimal (13% is 0.13).
  • Example with the class numbers: $375,000 ÷ 0.13 = $2,884,615. A plumbing business would need about $2.9 million in revenue to reach $375,000 in profit, if its profit percent were typical.
  • The average plumbing business had about $1.7 million in revenue. So an average one is too small for the class box. A buyer with that box needs a business that is bigger than average.

Then you answer one question about your first choice: does it look big enough? Your answer is one of three: it looks big enough, it does not, or I cannot tell. Any of the three can be right. The reasons are what count.

Warning signs. Module 4 gave you a danger list and the patterns behind it: high failure rate, cash trapped in inventory, heavy fixed costs, key-person risk, platform risk, and hidden liability. Check your first choice for those patterns. Then check three more signs that you can see from the outside:

  1. The same complaint shows up in many reviews.
  2. The business is not registered and active with the state. (INBiz, Indiana's business search, shows this.)
  3. Its hours or its services are getting smaller.

A warning sign is not a reason to stop. It is a reason to look closer.

The tech check. Look at how the business uses technology. Can a customer book online? Pay online? Does the business send reminders by text or email? Does it answer its reviews? Does it have social media accounts, and when was the last post? Some free website-checking pages, such as Similarweb, also list the tools a website uses. Missing technology is not a bad sign for a buyer. It can be the gap.
