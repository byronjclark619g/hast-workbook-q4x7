The Brief (read this first)

Think about looking for a house on a home-search website. When you first open it, you see every house for sale in the area — thousands of them. Nobody can look at thousands of houses. So you turn on the filters: three bedrooms, close to work, built after 2015, a big back yard. Now there are only a few dozen houses to look at. Codie Sanchez — whose book this course uses ideas from — says buying a business works the same way. You need filters, and you need to make them narrow.

Those filters are called your buy box: a written list of the exact kind of business you want to buy. Sanchez says she wasted many hours before she had one, because she looked at every business that anyone sent her. With a buy box, you can tell people exactly what you are looking for — and you can say "no" fast to the businesses that don't fit.

What goes in the box. Sanchez's list has a dozen items. This course sorts them into three groups:

  • About you: the area where the business is (city or region); the type of business; owner or operator — will you work in the business every day (operator), or hire someone to run it while you own it (owner)?; and one business or many.
  • About the money: how much the business sells in a year (revenue range); how much profit it makes; its margin; the price you would pay; and the multiple. (Her list also has "money down" — the cash a buyer pays at the start of the deal. This module leaves that one out.)
  • About the seller: what kind of seller you want. Sanchez wants a motivated seller — someone who really wants to sell, and who cares about more than getting the highest possible price.

Two of those money words are new:

  • Margin = profit ÷ revenue, written as a percent. A business that sells $1,000,000 and keeps $250,000 as profit has a 25% margin. It keeps 25 cents of every dollar it sells.
  • Multiple = asking price ÷ yearly profit. It tells you how many years of profit the price equals. A business with $200,000 profit that asks $600,000 has a multiple of 3.

A note on words. Business-for-sale listings usually say cash flow instead of profit. In this module, treat cash flow as the profit that is available each year to pay the owner, the operator, and any loan.

The money worksheet. Sanchez gives a four-step worksheet that turns your goals into numbers:

  1. Target income = your pay + the operator's pay.
  2. Loan payments = target income × 0.25. Most buyers borrow part of the price, and the loan has to be paid back every year. Sanchez's worksheet adds one-fourth of your target income to cover those yearly payments. This is her planning rule, not a bank rule.
  3. Total target profit = target income + loan payments. This is the least profit the business must make each year.
  4. Price range = total target profit × 2 (a likely low price) and × 4 (a likely high price).

So the classroom buy box needs a business that makes at least $375,000 profit a year, at a price of about $750,000 to $1,500,000. Sanchez adds a warning: do not worry too much about the price number. The buyer and seller can agree on a lower price. Profit is the most important number.

Sanchez's own buy box. Here is what she tells people she is looking for:

  • Revenue of $1 million to $20 million. Above $20 million, she would be competing with big investment companies.
  • A profit margin of 30 to 50 percent, and a history of profits every year. She does not want a business that needs fixing.
  • A simple business. She likes online businesses, service businesses (for homes and for offices), and businesses that come with property.
  • A motivated seller — not someone who only wants the highest price.
  • Owner, not operator. She hires an operator or pays the seller to run the business. She says she will not buy a business anymore until she has the operator first.
  • At least $300,000 to $350,000 profit, because she wants to pay herself $120,000, pay her operator $100,000, and keep extra money saved for problems.

Keep it narrow. Sanchez says her own box is wide, because she has bought many businesses. If you are just starting, keep your box narrow. Her example: a person who has worked in the cleaning industry looks only for a cleaning business, only in their own hometown. Keep it simple. Look for plain, easy-to-understand businesses with a record of profits.

Look at many to buy one. A buy box does not find a business in one day. Sanchez's recommendation: for every business you buy, look at about 100 businesses quickly, look at 50 more closely (for example, ask to see their financial records), and study 10 in deep detail — to find the 1 you should buy. She writes that in her group of more than a thousand business buyers, the average buyer looks at more than 30 deals and starts with a buy box that lists more than 20 companies (page 62). She also says the best way to know whether a deal is good or bad is to compare it to other deals. Your buy box is what makes those comparisons fast and fair.
