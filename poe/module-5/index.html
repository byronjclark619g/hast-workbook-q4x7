The Brief (read this first)

Here is a question new buyers ask all the time: "What's the best business to own?"

It's the wrong question. There is no such thing as the best business — there is only the right business for you. A business that would make one person rich and happy would make another person miserable and broke. Picking a business that does not fit who you are — how you like to work, what you're good at, what kind of life you want — is one of the most expensive mistakes a person can make.

Imagine this: you find a great manufacturing company for sale in your hometown. Good price, strong profits, and the owner wants to sell. Should you buy it? Not until you compare the deal to yourself. Say you like to think about big ideas but do not like small details. You like to travel, so you are hardly ever in your hometown. You are good at picturing things, but not at building things. Then that "great deal" would make you unhappy for years. Knowing yourself well enough to walk away from a good business that's wrong for you is a very valuable skill.

So before you judge another business, you're going to map yourself. Codie Sanchez — whose book this course uses ideas from — teaches a map with three circles:

Circle 1: Passion. What can you do for hours without getting bored? When did you last focus on something so hard that you forgot what time it was? Passion matters because owning is hard (Module 2 taught you that) — and you will not keep going through the hard parts of something you secretly hate. List what you love doing, even if it does not look like a business idea.

Circle 2: Experience and skills. What are you better at than most people you know? Not world-class — just better than average. Here is some good news about skills. Sanchez uses an idea from the writer Scott Adams called the skill stack: being in the top 1 percent at any one thing is nearly impossible. But top 10 percent at three or four things? Lots of people can do that — and the combination is rare. Someone who is good at organizing, good with people, and good with technology has a combination that almost nobody else has. Your strength is not one skill. It is your own combination of skills.

Circle 3: Network. Who do you know? Family, neighbors, coaches, your boss at a part-time job, the family friend who runs a shop. Sanchez suggests imagining a boardroom table — the big table where a company's advisers sit. Put a real person who could help you someday in each chair: there's my uncle who does drywall, there's my coach who knows everybody, there's my cousin who's great with money. They don't need to know they're at your table. Think broadly — you probably know more people than you remember at first.

Pay attention where the circles overlap. Sanchez tells the story of John, who mapped himself. His passion: building things and helping his community. His skills: logistics (keeping work on schedule), leadership, and technology. In his network: a brother-in-law who is a plumber and is always busy. John had never thought of plumbing as his opportunity until the map showed him. John's skills — organizing work and using technology — were what his brother-in-law's plumbing work was missing. And his brother-in-law had what John was missing: the plumbing skills.

One warning. When you list your skills, do NOT write "I'm great at turning bad situations around" and then convince yourself to buy a failing business. Saving a failing business is very hard, even for experts, and for most people who try it, it goes badly. Module 4 taught the same rule: beginners should not go looking for exceptions.

Last step: know what you want from owning. Sanchez points out that big investment companies judge deals with a checklist: cash flow, growth, how much they can sell it for later. That is fine for them — but you are not an investment company. You're a person building a life. So your map ends with an ownership goals statement: what would make this a win for you, how many hours you actually want to work, and what parts of your life you will not give up. A business that makes money but leaves you no time for anything else is not a win. Time for the rest of your life is part of the goal, not an extra.
