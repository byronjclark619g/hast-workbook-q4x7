The Brief (read this first)

Meet Mary. (Not her real name — her story is real, told by Codie Sanchez, whose book this course uses ideas from, about a member of her business community.) Mary bought her first business, and within months she was losing money, fighting with her business partner, and facing a lawsuit — with about $100,000 of her own money at risk.

Count her mistakes. Mistake one: she bought a restaurant — a franchise that was already losing money. Mistake two: she split ownership 50-50 with a partner she had never done business with, who put in no money but got his half on day one. Mistake three: they hired an inexperienced manager to run it. And one more problem: the restaurant was a three-hour flight from Mary's home.

Here's the painful part: even if Mary had fixed mistakes two and three, she probably still loses. Some business categories fail so often, or carry such serious hidden problems, that experienced buyers simply stay away from them. They don't argue about it. Buyers who succeed for many years keep asking one question — if I put my money into this business, what are the odds I get it back, plus more?

The danger list. Sanchez — a buyer of many small businesses — keeps a list of seven categories she will not buy. What matters more than her list is the risk pattern inside each one — because patterns show up in other places too.

  1. Restaurants. People love them, and they are very hard to run. Food spoils, what customers like changes, the profit on each sale is small, keeping enough workers is a daily problem — and banks are known for not wanting to lend money for restaurants, which tells you what the professionals think of the odds. Pattern: high failure rate.
  2. Hotels. They look like businesses, but they act like real estate with many complicated parts — huge buildings, huge loans, constant repairs, customers who need help at 3 a.m. And they often contain a restaurant. Pattern: asset-heavy (the business must own expensive buildings and equipment), complex.
  3. Retail storefronts. A shop must buy its products up front, months before customers maybe buy them — cash stuck in boxes in a stockroom. Add high rent for a busy location, and the problem gets worse: the store must pay money out every month, but money coming in is not guaranteed. Rent and insurance are examples of fixed costs — costs that stay the same whether you sell a lot or nothing at all. High fixed costs are what make a busy-looking store able to lose money every single month. Pattern: cash trapped in inventory + heavy fixed costs.
  4. Consulting firms. Some companies are really just one talented person with helpers. When the top consultant — the one all the clients actually came for — leaves, the clients leave too. This is key-person risk: the business's value depends on one person, and you cannot own a person. Buying a business like that isn't buying a business; it's buying someone else's job. Pattern: key-person risk.
  5. Personal brands. The extreme version of the same problem. A famous person's channel, page, or name can't truly be handed to you — the fans followed the person. Pattern: key-person risk at its highest level.
  6. Platform-dependent sellers. Some businesses exist entirely inside one giant company's website — every customer, every sale, every price goes through a platform the owner doesn't control. One rule change, one account suspension (even an unfair one), and a business with strong sales can be out of business in a week. This is platform risk: someone else owns the place where your business operates. Pattern: platform risk.
  7. Businesses with toxic cleanup problems. A dry cleaner can look clean and steady, but it may have used chemicals on its property for decades — and if the property needs environmental cleanup, the cost can be much larger than everything the business ever earned. The danger is a hidden liability: a cost you may have to pay that does not show up in the price. Pattern: hidden liability.

The rule: avoid businesses with some combination of high failure rates, key-person risk, platform risk, trapped cash, heavy fixed costs, and hidden liabilities. There are exceptions — there are always exceptions — but beginners should not go looking for exceptions. Beginners do best when they respect probabilities — they choose what usually works.

Task 1 — Name the Risk Pattern

For each mini-case below, name the risk pattern from the Brief that fits best, and explain your pick in one or two sentences. (Some cases could fit two patterns — pick the strongest one and defend it.)

Mini-cases (all made up for practice):

Case 1

A tutoring company where nearly every family signed up specifically for Ms. Rivera, the founder. She's thinking about moving away.

Case 2

An online store that sells only through one giant marketplace. Its account was suspended by mistake last year for 3 weeks.

Case 3

A boutique that still has $40,000 of last winter's coats in the stockroom — in April.

Case 4

A dry cleaner for sale cheap. The seller mentions the "old equipment room" hasn't been inspected in 30 years.

Case 5

A beautiful 60-room hotel with a huge monthly loan payment, constant repairs, and a diner inside that loses money.

Case 6

A new restaurant in the most popular location downtown. Full every weekend — and the rent is very high.
