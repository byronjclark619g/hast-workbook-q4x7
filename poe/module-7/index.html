The Brief (read this first)

In Module 6 you wrote down what business you want to buy. This module asks a different question: why?

Sanchez's why. Before Codie Sanchez — whose book this course uses ideas from — owned any businesses, she worked as a news reporter in Juárez, Mexico. The hard things she saw there taught her a lesson: money is power. So she went to work at big financial companies to learn how people build wealth. Then she started buying businesses herself. She writes that she would rather help a huge number of ordinary people get rich together than help a small group of people who are already rich get even richer (page 22). That is her why: the reason behind her work.

Her why also shows in her company's mission. In her words, the mission is "to transform America into a nation of owners so we can save our country's Main Street businesses, and so you never have to work for an insufferable boss again" (page 22). In plain words: help more people own businesses, keep small local businesses alive, and help people stop working for bad bosses.

Three words for this module.

  • Your why: the reason you want to do the work. If your first answer is "money," ask one more question: what is the money for?
  • Mission: a short statement of what a business does, who it helps, and why it matters. A mission is about today.
  • Vision: a description of where you want to be, years from now. A vision can be about a business (its future) or about you (your life as an owner). This module calls them your business vision and your personal vision.

The three checks for a mission. A mission statement should answer three questions:

  1. What does the business do?
  2. Who does it help, and how?
  3. Why does it matter?

Two real examples. Many businesses post their mission or vision on their website. These two were read on the businesses' own websites on October 15, 2026.

  • IKEA (the furniture store): IKEA's vision is "To create a better everyday life for the many people." IKEA calls its mission a "business idea": "to offer a wide range of well-designed, functional home furnishing products at prices so low that as many people as possible will be able to afford them." IKEA's website explains the difference this way: "While our vision tells us why we exist, our business idea tells us what we want to achieve."
  • Strack & Van Til (a grocery company that started in Highland, Indiana, in 1959): "Our mission is to provide an outstanding shopping experience through exceptional service, clean stores with product variety, and solutions that will continually evolve to meet the needs of our customers' and the communities we serve." (Continually evolve means keep changing over time.)

A made-up example: Ana's laundromat. Ana Ruiz and her business are made up. Ana grew up in Hammond. Her family had no washing machine, so every Saturday her mother carried the family's laundry to a laundromat on the other side of town and spent most of the day there. Last year Ana bought a laundromat near the street where she grew up, and she renamed it Ruiz Family Laundry. Here is what she wrote:

  • Ana's why: "Families in my neighborhood should not have to spend a whole Saturday washing clothes."
  • Ana's personal vision: "In ten years, I own three laundromats, I work about 40 hours a week, and I am free on Saturdays to watch my kids play soccer."
  • Ana's mission: "Ruiz Family Laundry gives Hammond families a clean, safe laundromat close to home, open early and late, so laundry takes an hour instead of a whole day."
  • Ana's business vision: "In five years, every family in our part of Hammond can walk to a clean laundromat."

Test Ana's mission with the three checks. What does the business do? It runs a clean, safe laundromat close to home, open early and late. Who does it help? Hammond families. Why does it matter? So laundry takes an hour instead of a whole day. Her mission passes all three checks.

Why a mission matters inside a business. Every Monday, Sanchez starts a short meeting with her team. The first two minutes are about her company's mission: each week she shares a real message from a customer whose life the company changed. She writes: "If you believe your people are only motivated by money, you do not understand what it means to be an owner yet" (page 183). In other words, the people who work for you want to know that their work helps people, not only what it pays.

Start with why: an idea from Simon Sinek. Simon Sinek is a writer and speaker. In 2009 he gave a talk called "How Great Leaders Inspire Action." In it, he says that every person and every business knows what they do. Some know how they do it — what makes them different or better. But very few know why they do it. He says that making money is not a why: "That's a result. It's always a result." By "why," he means your purpose: why your business exists, and why anyone should care.

Sinek says most businesses talk in this order: what, then how. Many never talk about why. He says the leaders and businesses that people want to follow talk in the opposite order: why, then how, then what. His short way to say it is: "People don't buy what you do; they buy why you do it." In plain words: customers choose a business because of what it believes, not only because of what it sells. He says the same thing about hiring: the goal is to hire people who believe what you believe, not just people who need a job.

Here is Ana's message to customers in both orders:

  • What first: "We have 40 washers and 30 dryers, and we are open from 6 a.m. to 11 p.m. Come wash with us."
  • Why first: "We believe no family should have to spend a whole Saturday on laundry. So we keep our laundromat clean, safe, close to home, and open early and late. We have 40 washers and 30 dryers. Come wash with us."

The facts are the same. Only the order changed. Sinek's point is that the same facts sound different when the why comes first.
