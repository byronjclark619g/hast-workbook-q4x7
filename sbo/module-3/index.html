The Brief (read this first)

First impressions matter. After an ownership change, employees remember everything about the new owner's first meeting — every word, every tone. That can go very wrong. In 2023, a new CEO at a Utah company called Clearlink held his first big company-wide video meeting and used it to tell over a thousand employees how hard HE works, challenge "any one of you to outwork me" — adding "but you won't" — and accuse the team of not working hard. The meeting was so bad it went viral; hundreds of thousands of strangers watched clips of it online. Imagine being on that team, deciding in minute one whether to trust the new boss. Employees decide that fast. He gave them every reason to say no.

The handoff, done right. Your first meeting at Calumet has two speakers, and the first one isn't you. Gus opens. The old owner's job: be positive, be proud, tell the whole team that you are the new owner and that he supports you — and be brief, then gone. Brief means brief: Gus talks for five minutes at most, then he leaves the room. The whole meeting — Gus, you, and a few questions — should run about 20 minutes. Any longer and it stops being a handoff and starts being a lecture. The crew's loyalty is to Gus; his public support moves some of that loyalty to you. Then it's your turn, and your whole speech needs only four ingredients:

  1. Energy. Businesses die when people stop caring. You're bringing new energy, and you're excited to help this team win.
  2. Humility. You're here to learn. Say the words your team most wants to hear: no big changes right away. You bought Calumet because it works — you intend to learn why before touching anything. (You now know this is also literally your Module 2 plan. Saying it out loud is free, and it earns a lot of trust.)
  3. Excitement about THEM. Why did you buy this business? Say it. Where is it headed? Describe it — and describe their place in it. A smart step: learn every name and role BEFORE the meeting, and use them. "Denise runs this place better than I ever will" costs you one sentence and earns you an ally.
  4. Gratitude. They stayed. Say thank you. Tell them your door is open, and mean it.

Then stop talking. Keep the whole thing short; let people bring questions afterward — people say what they really feel one-on-one, not in a group.

What the four ingredients sound like together. Here is a new owner's speech at a different business — a made-up auto repair shop — with each ingredient labeled. Notice how short it is. Yours will be about Calumet, in your words, with Calumet's real names.

Energy "Good morning. I'm Morgan Reyes, and as of this morning I'm the owner of Lakeside Auto Care. I've wanted to be part of a shop like this for a long time, and I'm excited to be here with you.

Humility Here's the most important thing I'll say today: no big changes right away. I bought this shop because it works. My job for the next couple of months is to learn why it works — from you — before I touch anything.

Excitement about THEM I bought Lakeside because of the people in this room. Pat, customers ask for you by name at the front counter. Luis, I've seen the reviews about your diagnostic work. Kim, I know the parts room runs because you run it. Where this shop goes next, it goes with you.

Gratitude You stayed through a sale. That means a lot. Thank you.

What happens next This week I'll hand each of you a short two-question survey. It's private — between you and me. Then I'll sit down with each of you one-on-one. My door is open starting now."

That's about 170 words. Then the new owner stops talking.

The listening system. A speech is you talking. Now do the opposite: you listen. Within the first week, give every employee a short written survey — just two questions:

Blockers: What gets in the way of doing your job well? What do you need that you don't have?
Ideas: If you owned this place, what would you change or try? What would make this business better?

Give a clear deadline (one week), and promise — truthfully — that answers stay between each employee and you. Then meet with each person, one-on-one, to talk through what they wrote. This little system does three big things at once: it hands you a map of problems you'd take months to find alone; it shows the team you're a listener, not a lecturer (watch people move along the Module 2 change curve — shock → anger → resentment → acceptance → hope — because you asked instead of told); and it quietly shows you who's hopeful and engaged versus who may already be thinking about quitting. The answers are very valuable. The act of asking is worth even more.

Your plan, in writing. The last piece of this module turns Module 2's "Days 1–60: learn and document" window into an actual written plan — the kind real operators are handed on day one. The format is simple on purpose (simple plans get used; complicated plans get put away and forgotten):

Focus — one sentence: what these 30 days are FOR.
Priorities — the two or three things that matter most.
Learning goals (at least two) — what you must come to understand, each with a metric: how you'll know you learned it.
Performance goals (at least two) — what must actually get done, each with a metric.

At day 30, you review it: what got done, what didn't, what changed. Then you write the next 30. That rhythm — plan, work, review, repeat — is the habit every well-run business keeps, and it starts here.
