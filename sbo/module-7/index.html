The Brief (read this first)

What you can spend. In Module 1 you found Calumet's profit for last year: $520,000 − $442,000 = $78,000. Gus's own pay, $50,000, was inside the wages. You do his job now, so that money is yours. You also pay Gus $2,600 a month, which is $31,200 a year. So this is the money you have for the year: $78,000 + $50,000 − $31,200 = $96,800. Prices stay the same for now, so any raise comes out of this $96,800.

One dollar an hour. Calumet's crew works 40 hours a week, 50 weeks a year. That is 2,000 hours. So $1 more an hour for one worker costs $2,000 a year. That is about 2% of your $96,800. Real businesses also pay taxes on wages, in addition to the pay itself. To keep the math simple, this course leaves those taxes out of Calumet's numbers this year.

Three checks for pay. A pay number needs reasons behind it. Check every pay number three ways:

  1. Inside the business. Who earns what, and does the order make sense? A crew lead should earn more than the people she leads and trains.
  2. Against the market. What do other businesses pay for the same job?
  3. Against the money. What does it cost in a year, and how much of the $96,800 is that?

A raise or lead pay? There are two ways to pay someone more for leading. A raise makes the person's pay higher, and it stays higher. Lead pay is extra pay per hour that comes with the lead job. Many businesses pay crew leads this way. For Tina's trial, the difference matters. Module 2 says changes in this window should be small, test-sized, and reversible: you can undo them. If the trial does not work, lead pay ends with the lead job, and Tina goes back to her crew job at her crew pay. Everyone knows that from the start. Taking back a raise is much harder, and it hurts the worker.

More than pay. Codie Sanchez, the business buyer whose book this course draws on, writes: "My rule is always be generous in paying those who make your life easier" (page 154). She also says money should not be the only thing you offer. She gives three extras (page 154):

  1. A performance bonus: extra money for reaching a clear goal. Her example: if the manager grows the business's sales by $100,000 in a year, the manager gets a 5% bonus, which is $5,000.
  2. Equity: a small part of the ownership of the business. Her example: after one year of meeting the goals, the manager earns 2% of the business. She says this works best with people who have already proven themselves.
  3. Payouts for staying: extra money when the person reaches three, five, and ten years at the business.

Her examples are for a manager who runs the whole business for the owner. Calumet does not have one. You run it yourself (Module 4). For a crew lead, the extras have to fit the job and the money.

The parts of an offer. When Sanchez hires a manager, she writes the offer as a short table. She gives three bonuses: one for profit, one for sales, and one for people (page 168). Each bonus has a goal written next to it. This is one of her offers (pages 168–169):

Why this works: A pay number with no reasons behind it is a guess. When you check pay inside the business, against the market, and against the money, you can explain every number to the worker, to the rest of the crew, and to yourself. Lead pay lets you test a new leader without cutting anyone's pay later. And an offer with clear goals tells the person what the job is and how their work will be judged.
