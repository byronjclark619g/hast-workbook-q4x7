The Brief (read this first)

A bad hire is one of the most expensive mistakes an owner makes. Not just the wages — the redone work, the customers who notice, the good employees who get tired of doing someone else's share of the work, the months lost before you admit the mistake and start over. Most owners hire on gut feeling: a nice interview, a firm handshake, "I had a good feeling about him." Gut feelings are how bad hires happen. Owners who hire well use a system, and the system starts before any candidate shows up.

First: what does a great hire look like? Codie Sanchez — the business buyer whose book this course draws on — describes the person worth looking for in phrases like these: completely honest. A history of winning — at something, anywhere. Someone who leads instead of waits — sees the empty trailer and loads it without being told. Someone who takes work away from you, not someone who becomes more work for you. And where you can find it: someone other people would follow.

The three filters. Before scoring anyone, run three questions:

  1. Known? Do you — or does someone you trust — actually know this person? There's an old story told about Winston Churchill: showing a new member of Parliament around, the young man pointed across the chamber at the other party and said "so that's the enemy." Churchill corrected him: the opposition sits across from you — the enemy sits behind you. The point for an owner: your biggest risks are inside your business, not outside it. Every hire is someone you're placing behind you. Known people — vouched for by someone whose judgment you trust (to vouch for someone means to say, from your own experience, that the person is good) — are risks you can measure. With a total stranger, you are only guessing.
  2. Experienced? Have they done this actual job before — not something like it, this? Every skill a hire already has is a skill you don't have to pay to teach while they practice on your customers.
  3. Proven? The best predictor of future behavior is past behavior. Leadership and reliability leave a record you can check: teams captained, projects finished, jobs they kept and moved up in, people who'd work with them again. One revealing interview question: "If you came to work here, is there anyone from a past job you'd want to bring along?" If they can't name anyone, ask yourself — was anyone actually following them?

There is one real trade-off inside these filters: proven talent costs more; potential talent costs training. A candidate with years of exactly-this-job experience can ask for higher pay. A promising beginner is cheaper but becomes YOUR job to train — hours of teaching, checking, and fixing. Neither answer is wrong. Owners just have to know which one they're choosing, on purpose.

The scorecard. Now the tool. Instead of a gut feeling, score every candidate 0–5 in five categories, for a total out of 25:

CategoryThe question it asks
KnownDoes someone we trust vouch for this person? (5 = trusted teammate vouches strongly; 0 = total stranger; nobody you trust knows them)
Proven experienceHave they done this exact job before?
Proven industryHave they worked in this industry or a closely related one?
Proven problem setHave they dealt with the specific problems THIS job is about?
Proven scaleHave they worked somewhere about our size, where the job looked like it looks here?

Here's what it looks like used on three candidates for an office-operations job (practice example): Rosa scores 24/25 — a trusted colleague vouches for her, she's done the exact job, same industry, solved the exact problem before, at a company just our size. Dev scores 17/25 — solid experience, but the person vouching for him only heard about him from someone else, and his industry is related to ours, not the same. Pat scores 1/25 — nobody knows them, never done the role, wrong industry, wrong scale. A gut feeling might have hired Pat after a great interview. The scorecard will not.

Two rules make the scorecard work: edit the categories to fit the job — the five above are the standard set, but "the problems THIS job is about" changes with every opening (for a mowing-crew hire, reliability might BE the problem set). And score with evidence, not impressions — every number should have a "because" behind it.
