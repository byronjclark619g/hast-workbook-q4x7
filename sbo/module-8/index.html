The Brief (read this first)

Old prices. Gus's list in your case file says: "Prices haven't changed since 2022. People love a deal. Also I hated that phone call." Codie Sanchez, the business buyer whose book this course draws on, writes that most small businesses are three to five years behind in price increases (page 208). Costs did not stay the same in those years. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics is a government office that tracks prices. Its numbers show that prices in the United States rose about 15% from 2022 to September 2026. Something that cost $38 in 2022 costs about $43.75 now. So a $38 cut pays for less gas, fewer parts, and less of the crew's wages than it did in 2022.

A price analysis. Before Sanchez buys a business, she studies its prices. She asks five questions (pages 212–213):

  1. What are the current prices?
  2. When were they last raised?
  3. How often are they raised?
  4. What do competitors charge?
  5. What do similar services sell for in the market?

She writes that after this study, an owner can usually raise prices by 5% to 30% (page 213).

What a raise can do. Sanchez gives an example of a pressure-washing business (pages 207–208). In one year, 500 people asked for a price. Out of every 100, 35 said yes. That is 175 jobs at $400 each, or $70,000. Then the owner raises the price 20%, to $480. Now 33 out of every 100 say yes. That is 165 jobs at $480 each, or $79,200. The business lost 10 jobs and earned $9,200 more. A raise will lose some customers. The question to answer first is how many you can lose.

What others charge. Two websites match homeowners with lawn companies. Both show prices for Hammond. LawnGuru lists $37.37 to $46.05 for a cut with trimming, edging, and blowing. GreenPal says most Hammond homes pay $35 to $50. These are Hammond prices. Calumet also mows in Highland and Munster, and we did not find listings for those towns. You will look for one in Part A.

Lots are not the same size. Town records show this. In one Hammond neighborhood built in the 1950s, 93 of the 101 lots are 59 feet by 102 feet. That is about 6,000 square feet. Highland's town code says a new house lot must be at least 7,200 square feet, and at least 10,000 in its large-lot areas. A bigger lot usually has more lawn, and more lawn takes more minutes.

Dollars per crew-hour. A lawn company sells its crew's time. Two people work at every Calumet stop. One crew-hour is one hour of one worker's time. Dollars per crew-hour tells you how much money comes in for each hour of crew time:

  • money ÷ crew-hours = dollars per crew-hour

Here is one stop as an example. It pays $40. It takes two people 16 minutes, counting the drive to the next stop. That is 32 minutes of crew time. $40 ÷ 32 = $1.25 for each crew-minute, and $1.25 × 60 = $75 per crew-hour. A higher number means the crew's time earns more.

Your case file says the crew is paid for 40 hours a week all year. So when Calumet drops one lawn, the wages do not go down. The crew has more free time, and that time earns nothing until Calumet sells it to another customer.

Homes and commercial accounts. Calumet has 168 homes with no contracts and 9 commercial accounts with yearly contracts. A commercial job is priced by a bid. The company looks at the site and offers a price for each visit or for the whole season. You read real bids on Research Day. In 2024, the city of La Porte got two prices to mow one city property, Beechwood Lakes: $750 per mowing from K&K Outdoor Services, and $900 per mowing with a spring cleanup from Mofield Property Services. The city chose Mofield, the higher price, for quality. Bids can also be very close. In 2026, La Porte got two prices for a year of work at another property, NewPorte Landing: $25,022 from Lakeshore Landscaping and about $25,068 from MP Lawn Care.

A contract means the work is promised for a year. Your case file also shows a problem. One commercial customer, Riverbend, is about 22% of all of Calumet's revenue, and it pays in 60 days.

A price and a payment plan are two different things. A price change changes how much a customer pays. A payment plan changes when the money arrives. For Calumet, "when" matters a lot. In March, $10,000 comes in and $31,000 goes out. In November, $12,000 comes in and $30,000 goes out. Sanchez writes that an owner should "get paid in full, up front, and often, whenever and wherever you can" (page 214). She also suggests a discount for customers who pay up front (page 208). In this module you compare two plans:

  1. Per cut, in writing. This is today's deal, written down. The customer still pays after each cut. A written agreement says the season dates, the number of cuts, what each visit includes, what happens in a week when it rains, the price and how it can change, how to pay, and how either side can cancel.
  2. Pay up front. The customer pays for the whole season in March, before the first cut, and gets a small discount. Paragon Landscape, a company in Harlan, Indiana, gives customers 2% to 3% off for paying for the year ahead.

Here is the pay-up-front math for one customer as an example. The price is $40 a cut, and the season has 28 cuts. $40 × 28 = $1,120. With 2% off, the discount is $22.40, so the customer pays $1,097.60 in March. Calumet gets the money early. It also owes that customer 28 cuts.

Task 2 — Test a price on paper

Choose Highland or Munster. Choose a new price for that town. Sanchez's range is 5% to 30% more. Then find how many customers could leave before Calumet earns less from that town.

Here is the method, with Hammond as the example. Hammond brings in 100 × $35 = $3,500 a week. Suppose the new price is $38. $3,500 ÷ $38 = 92.1, so Calumet needs 93 customers to earn at least the same money. 100 − 93 = 7. Seven customers could leave.
