The Brief (read this first)

The interview. Many owners interview with a friendly talk. They ask whatever questions they think of, and they hire the person they liked best. Codie Sanchez, the business buyer whose book this course draws on, interviews a different way. She calls it a high-standard interview, and it has three parts (page 166):

  1. It uses the same questions every time, for every applicant.
  2. The answers are written down, so other people on the team can read them. At Calumet, that could be Denise or Ray.
  3. Each applicant is scored on the scorecard categories.

Her advice is simple: plan the questions before the interview, and do not change them. When every applicant answers the same question, you can compare the answers. When you ask each person something different, you can only compare how much you liked them.

Calumet's six questions. Sanchez builds her interview questions from her company's values, the things her company cares about most (page 166). Calumet does not have written values, so its questions come from what this job needs. Calumet asks every applicant these six questions:

  1. Tell me about your last two jobs. What did you do, and what is one thing you did well? Sanchez asks applicants to go over their past jobs one by one and name what they achieved (page 166). This question shows what the person has really done.
  2. If I call your last boss, what will that person say you do well? What will they say you need to work on? Sanchez writes: "There's always a real possibility that you WILL call these people. You're more likely to get an honest answer by mentioning the possibility" (page 166). So at the start of each interview, you tell the applicant that you might call their past bosses.
  3. Tell me about a time something went wrong at work. What happened? What would you do differently? Sanchez asks what should have been different on a past project. She watches whether the person blames other people, or can see their own part in the problem (page 167).
  4. This job runs all year: mowing in summer, plowing and salting in winter, often very early in the morning, and shop work in between. How does that fit your life? This question is not from the book. It is Calumet's own question. It checks whether the person can do the job as it really is.
  5. Where do you want to be in two years? Sanchez looks for goals that fit the job and the company (page 168).
  6. What did you do to get ready for this interview? What do you know about Calumet? Sanchez asks how applicants prepared, because the answer shows how they think (page 167).

Good signs and warning signs. Sanchez lists answers that are good signs and answers that are warning signs (pages 167–168). In plain words:

Good signs:

  • A detailed description of the work they did
  • Specific things they did well or made better
  • A clear understanding of how their work helped the team or the company
  • Examples from past jobs that show what this job needs
  • Goals that fit the job and the company
  • Clear plans for the future that could really happen

Warning signs:

  • A vague description of past jobs
  • Cannot say what they did or what they did well
  • Talks only about the bad parts of past jobs
  • Answers that sound memorized or say very little
  • Goals that have nothing to do with the job or the company
  • Plans that are vague, or too big to happen in this job

Reference calls. A reference is a past boss, or another person who has seen the applicant work. A reference call lets you check what the applicant told you. The best guide to how a person will work is how they have worked before.

Score again. Sanchez's interview ends with a score for each applicant on the same categories as her scorecard (page 166). Calumet uses the scorecard from Module 4. Score every applicant 0–5 in each category, for a total out of 25:

No hire yet. About her scorecard, Sanchez writes: "It's amazing how many people this screens out" (page 164). She means the scorecard removes many applicants from the list. A bad hire is one of the most expensive mistakes an owner makes. If no applicant is good enough, "no hire yet" is a real decision. The next step is to find out why the right people did not apply, and to change something before the next group of applicants.

Task 3 — Make the decision

Tasks 1–3 are graded together using the Practical Application Rubric.

Why this works: A friendly talk tells you who you liked. The same questions, written notes, reference calls, and a scorecard tell you who can do the job. Owners who hire this way make fewer bad hires, and a bad hire is one of the most expensive mistakes an owner makes. When the evidence says yes, they hire with facts behind the decision. When the evidence says no, they have the facts to wait and a plan to find better applicants.
