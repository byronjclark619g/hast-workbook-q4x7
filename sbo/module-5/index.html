The Brief (read this first)

Make good workers want to come to you. Most job listings read like a list of chores and rules: "Duties include: mowing. Requirements: mowing experience." Nobody gets excited reading that. Here is a better way to think about it: a job listing is an advertisement. You are selling a real job to a stranger who has other choices — and good workers always have other choices. The businesses that bring in great people write listings that sell the job, honestly.

Honest selling works better than making the job sound perfect. There's a famous recruitment ad, often attributed to the polar explorer Ernest Shackleton — and here's a fact for this class: we could not confirm that he actually ran it, so treat it as a legend that teaches — a story that may not be true but still teaches something (notice how we just labeled that — that's what you do with a source you cannot confirm). The legend goes: "Men wanted for hazardous journey. Low wages, bitter cold, long hours of complete darkness. Safe return doubtful. Honour and recognition in case of success." Notice what it does NOT do: it doesn't pretend the job is easy. It tells the hard truth — and the right kind of person becomes more interested. An honest listing turns away the people who would quit in the second week and brings in the people who fit the real job. That is not a mistake. That is its purpose.

The seven parts of a listing that works:

  1. Write like you talk. Read your draft out loud. If it sounds stiff and formal ("we look forward to your presence"), rewrite it the way you would talk to a smart, curious friend. Honest words are better than fancy words, in every line.
  2. A plain title that is easy to search for. People search "lawn care crew member," not "Grass Ninja" (a joke title). Clever titles hide your job from the exact people looking for it.
  3. An opening that speaks to the reader. What has frustrated this person at other jobs — being treated as if they do not matter? No way to move up? Start there: show them this job fixes a problem they have had.
  4. What the job actually is. The real duties, plainly. The more skilled the role, the more specific you get.
  5. What the company is. How the business makes its money, in a sentence or two — and a bit of story. (Calumet has a real one: one man, one truck, one mower, 2009.)
  6. Where it's going. People want to join a business that is going somewhere, not only earn a paycheck. Write one or two honest sentences about the future.
  7. The practical details — including the pay. Location, schedule, benefits, how to apply, and the pay range, stated plainly. Codie Sanchez — the business buyer whose book this course draws on — says postings that show a pay range are much more likely to get applications than postings that hide it. Hiding the pay looks like a trick. Showing it looks like respect.

Beyond the posting: six ways to find good people. A listing waits for people to find it. Owners who hire well also go and find people:

  1. People already doing the job — for someone else. The best person you could hire is working on someone else's crew right now. There is nothing dishonest about letting good workers know a better job exists.
  2. Veterans. Sanchez strongly recommends hiring people who served in the military — disciplined, comfortable with structure, calm decision-makers under pressure — and she admits she may be biased (she is married to a veteran). Veteran job programs exist specifically to connect veterans with work, which tells you plenty of employers agree with her.
  3. Recruiters. Professionals whose whole job is knowing who is good and available. They cost money — but paying to use the contacts someone built over many years is often worth it.
  4. Keep a list of skilled people (competence collecting). Every time someone gives you service that is better than you expected — the worker at the parts counter who solves your problem, the fast-food shift leader whose team works smoothly — write down the name and how to reach that person. Keep adding to the list. One day you will need to hire, and the list will be ready.
  5. Help other people find workers. When a friend asks "do you know anyone for a job?" — take ten minutes and really try to help. People remember who helped them hire. Later, they help you.
  6. What people can see of your business online. A business that posts its work — before-and-after photos, a crew that looks good to work with — has a free job advertisement that never comes down. (Calumet is not on the internet at all. Now you can see that this hurts hiring too.)

One sentence connects this whole module to your future as an owner: the money a business makes is limited by the good people it can attract. Read that again. It is why hiring gets more than one module in this course and why owners never really stop looking for good people.
