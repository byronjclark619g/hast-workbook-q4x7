The Brief (read this first)

The most dangerous person in a just-sold business is a confident new owner. Gus ran Calumet for 17 years. You've owned it for a week. Walk in changing things you don't understand yet, and you will break systems you didn't know existed — the informal ones, the ones living in Denise's head and Ray's routines. The winning posture for a new owner is humility: assume you are the least-informed person in the building, because you are. Ask questions instead of giving feedback. Write down everything. As Codie Sanchez — the business buyer whose book this course draws on — puts it: you've got to know it before you grow it. New owners who charge in like a bull don't fix china shops — they wreck them.

The good news: the first months have a known shape. Sanchez has managed the handoff on a great many acquisitions; this is the timeline she teaches, and this year we'll use it too:

Days 1–60: Learn and document. Your whole job is to find out what you REALLY bought. Who does what? Where are the keys, the passwords, the paper files? Which customers are touchy, which mower is cursed, what does Denise actually do all day (spoiler: more than the job title says)? You follow, you watch, you write down. You change nothing important.
Days 60–90: Stabilize and set standards. Now you start fixing what's clearly broken and writing down how things should run — so the business stops depending on memory. This window is where the business's habits start becoming written rules and routines. (Writing those properly is a skill of its own — it gets its own module later this year.)
Days 90–120: Power up. Three priorities: speed up how fast cash actually reaches the bank, identify the people the business truly depends on, and begin small changes — test-sized, reversible ones.
Day 120 and beyond: Run and build. Now you have your feet under you. The notebook full of ideas you've been keeping since day one? THIS is when it opens. Every idea you resisted in month one is still there — and now you know enough to tell the good ones from the grenades.

One honest warning: Sanchez calls the entire first year the riskiest — the one where new owners most often stumble. The timeline exists because of that. Plans beat guessing.

The other half: people. A business isn't machines — it's Denise, Ray, Tina, Marcus, and Sam, and every one of them just had their world changed without being asked. Humans process unwanted change in a known pattern — a change curve: first shock ("the business sold?!"), then anger ("nobody asked us"), then resentment ("the new owner's probably going to ruin it"), then acceptance ("okay, this is happening"), and finally, for most, hope ("actually… this could be good").

A true-to-life story from Sanchez's world: a landscaping-company buyer named Nolan called his first all-team meeting, excited to introduce himself. One minute in, an employee cut him off — "I don't like this. I quit" — and walked out. Three more quit by the end of the day. For two weeks, something went wrong daily. Then… it settled. People moved along the curve, the ones who stayed found reasons to hope, and the team stabilized. Sanchez — who has watched many of these handoffs — says the storm usually calms within a few paychecks, once people see the checks still clear and the new owner isn't a wrecking ball.

Two lessons from Nolan: expect the curve — it isn't a sign you've failed; it's what change does to people. And your behavior decides how fast people move through it. A humble, question-asking, nothing-breaking owner gives people reasons to slide toward hope. A know-it-all gives them reasons to park at anger.
